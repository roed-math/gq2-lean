# A proof reduction for David Roe’s presentation of \(G_{\mathbf Q_2}\)

## 1. Statement and status

Let \(\omega=\omega_2\in\widehat{\mathbf Z}\) be the idempotent whose
\(\mathbf Z_2\)-component is \(1\) and whose \(\mathbf Z_\ell\)-component is
\(0\) for \(\ell\ne2\).  Use
\[
g^h=h^{-1}gh,\qquad [g,h]=g^{-1}h^{-1}gh.
\]
Define
\[
\sigma_2=\sigma^\omega,\qquad
a=(x_0^{-3}\tau)^\omega,\qquad
c=[x_1,x_1^{\sigma_2}]
\]
and let \(\Gamma_R\) be the profinite group defined by
\[
\tau^\sigma=\tau^2,\qquad
r_R:=(x_0^\sigma)^{-1}a\,x_1^2c=1,
\]
with the additional requirement that the closed normal closure of
\(x_0,x_1\) be pro-\(2\).

The claim of this note is:

> **Theorem (relative to the results proved in the Roe–Turturean paper).**
> Replacing the paper’s candidate relator by \(r_R\), all source-side
> hypotheses of its finite-quotient induction still hold.  Consequently
> \[
> \Gamma_R\cong G_{\mathbf Q_2}.
> \]

Thus the work needed for a complete adaptation is confined to the
candidate-specific calculations in Sections 3, 5, and 6 of the paper.  The
boundary-framed induction, finite Fourier inversion, exact-image recursion,
and profinite reconstruction then apply unchanged.

## 2. Tame quotient and the integral unramified character

Kill the wild normal subgroup generated by \(x_0,x_1\).  In every finite
quotient, the relation \(\tau^\sigma=\tau^2\) forces \(\tau\) to have odd
order: conjugation preserves its order, while squaring preserves that order
only when it is odd.  Hence \(\tau^\omega=1\).  The second relation becomes
trivial, and the tame quotient is
\[
\mathcal T=\langle \sigma,\tau\mid \tau^\sigma=\tau^2\rangle.
\]

The assignment
\[
\nu_R(\sigma)=1,\qquad
\nu_R(\tau)=\nu_R(x_0)=\nu_R(x_1)=0
\]
defines a continuous epimorphism \(\Gamma_R\twoheadrightarrow\mathbf Z_2\).
It induces the usual unramified marking on the tame quotient.

This supplies the tame half of the paper’s boundary construction.

## 3. The fully marked maximal pro-\(2\) quotient

### 3.1 The pro-\(2\) relator

In a pro-\(2\) quotient the tame relation forces \(\tau=1\), since
squaring cannot be an automorphism of a nontrivial procyclic pro-\(2\)
group.  Also \(g^\omega=g\) for every pro-\(2\) element \(g\).  Put
\[
s=\sigma,\qquad x=x_0,\qquad y=x_1.
\]
The maximal pro-\(2\) quotient is therefore
\[
D_R=
\left\langle s,x,y\ \middle|\
r_2=(x^s)^{-1}x^{-3}y^2[y,y^s]=1
\right\rangle_{\mathrm{pro}-2}.
\]
Since \(x^s=x[x,s]\),
\[
r_2=[x,s]^{-1}x^{-4}y^2[y,y^s].
\]

Modulo the third Zassenhaus term, \(x^{-4}\) has degree at least \(4\) and
\([y,y^s]\) has degree at least \(3\).  Thus the degree-two initial form is
\[
y^2+[x,s].
\]
The corresponding cup–Bockstein matrix in the dual basis to \(s,x,y\) is
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix},
\]
which is nonsingular.  Hence \(D_R\) is a rank-three Demuškin group.

Its abelianization is
\[
B_R=D_R^{\mathrm{ab}}
=\langle \bar s,\bar x,\bar y\mid -4\bar x+2\bar y=0\rangle_{\mathbf Z_2}
\cong \mathbf Z_2^2\oplus C_2.
\]
Put
\[
t=\bar y-2\bar x.
\]
Then
\[
B_R=C_2t\oplus\mathbf Z_2\bar s\oplus\mathbf Z_2\bar x,
\qquad
\nu_R(t,\bar s,\bar x)=(0,1,0).
\]

### 3.2 Computing the canonical Demuškin orientation

This is the main new calculation.  Let \(\chi_R\) be the canonical
Demuškin orientation and write
\[
S=\chi_R(s),\qquad X=\chi_R(x),\qquad Y=\chi_R(y).
\]
Labute’s characterization says that every crossed derivation of the free
pro-\(2\) group into the rank-one module defined by \(\chi_R\) must descend
through \(r_2\).  If
\[
D(gh)=Dg+\chi_R(g)Dh,
\]
the coefficients of \(D(r_2)\) give
\[
\begin{aligned}
Y^2&=X^4,\\
0&=-(X^{-1}S^{-1}+X^{-2}+X^{-3}+X^{-4}),\\
0&=S^{-1}\left[-X^{-1}(X-1)+\frac{(Y-1)^2}{Y^2}\right],\\
0&=X^{-4}(1+Y)+Y^{-1}(1-Y^{-1})(S^{-1}-1).
\end{aligned}
\]
The branch \(Y=X^2\) gives no solution.  The other branch gives
\[
Y=-X^2,\qquad
X^3+2X^2+1=0,\qquad
S=-\frac{X^3}{X^2+X+1}.
\]

The polynomial \(f(Z)=Z^3+2Z^2+1\) has exactly one root in
\(\mathbf Z_2^\times\): modulo \(2\) the only possible root is \(1\), and
\(f'(1)\equiv1\pmod2\), so Hensel’s lemma applies.  More precisely,
\[
X\equiv5\pmod {16},\qquad S\equiv13\pmod {16}.
\]
Therefore
\[
v_2(X-1)=v_2(S-1)=2,
\]
so each of \(X,S\) topologically generates \(1+4\mathbf Z_2\).  Also
\[
\chi_R(t)=YX^{-2}=-1.
\]
It follows that
\[
\operatorname{im}\chi_R=\{\pm1\}\times(1+4\mathbf Z_2).
\]
Labute’s classification now identifies \(D_R\) with the rank-three,
\(q=2\), secondary-exponent-\(4\) group
\[
D_0=\langle A,S_0,Y_0\mid A^2S_0^4[S_0,Y_0]=1\rangle_{\mathrm{pro}-2},
\]
which is \(G_{\mathbf Q_2}(2)\).

### 3.3 Preserving the full \(\mathbf Z_2\)-marking

An unmarked isomorphism is not enough for the paper’s fibre product.  We
now construct the required isomorphism on abelianizations.

Because \(X\) and \(S\) both generate \(1+4\mathbf Z_2\), there is a unique
\(b\in\mathbf Z_2^\times\) such that
\[
S=X^b.
\]
Set
\[
k=\bar s-b\bar x.
\]
Then
\[
B_R=C_2t\oplus\mathbf Z_2k\oplus\mathbf Z_2\bar x,
\quad
\nu_R(t,k,\bar x)=(0,1,0),
\quad
\chi_R(t,k,\bar x)=(-1,1,X).
\]

For \(D_0\), put \(t_0=\bar A+2\bar S_0\) and
\(\eta=(-3)^{-1}\).  The paper proves
\[
D_0^{\mathrm{ab}}
=C_2t_0\oplus\mathbf Z_2\bar S_0\oplus\mathbf Z_2\bar Y_0,
\]
with
\[
\nu_0(t_0,\bar S_0,\bar Y_0)=(0,1,0),
\qquad
\chi_0(t_0,\bar S_0,\bar Y_0)=(-1,1,\eta).
\]
Both \(\eta\) and \(X\) generate \(1+4\mathbf Z_2\), so there is a unit
\(u\in\mathbf Z_2^\times\) with \(\eta^u=X\).  Hence
\[
t\longmapsto t_0,\qquad
k\longmapsto\bar S_0,\qquad
\bar x\longmapsto u\bar Y_0
\]
is an isomorphism \(B_R\to D_0^{\mathrm{ab}}\) preserving both \(\nu\) and
\(\chi\).

Choose any abstract isomorphism \(D_R\to D_0\).  Its abelianized map and
the displayed desired map both preserve the intrinsic orientation.  Their
difference is therefore an orientation-preserving automorphism of
\(D_0^{\mathrm{ab}}\).  By the paper’s Proposition 3.9, that automorphism
lifts to an automorphism of \(D_0\).  Correcting the abstract isomorphism
by this lift gives
\[
(D_R,\nu_R)\cong(G_{\mathbf Q_2}(2),\nu_{\mathrm{ur}})
\]
as fully marked pairs over the entire \(\mathbf Z_2\), not merely modulo
\(2\).

## 4. Evaluated Fox Jacobian

Let \(V\) be a simple characteristic-\(2\) module for a finite lower tame
quotient.  Write the actions of \(\sigma,\tau,\sigma_2\) as
\[
\mathsf S,\qquad \mathsf T,\qquad
\mathsf U=\mathsf S^\omega.
\]
Let
\[
P=1+\mathsf T+\cdots+\mathsf T^{e-1}
\]
be the tame-inertia norm projector, and write lift variables on
\((\sigma,\tau,x_0,x_1)\) as \((a,b,c,d)\).

The tame row is the paper’s unchanged row
\[
L_{\mathrm t}
=\mathsf S^{-1}(1+\mathsf T)a+
(\mathsf S^{-1}+1+\mathsf T)b.
\]
For Roe’s wild word:

- \((x_0^\sigma)^{-1}\) contributes \(\mathsf S^{-1}c\);
- \((x_0^{-3}\tau)^\omega\) contributes \(P(c+b)\), since
  \(-3\equiv1\pmod2\);
- \(x_1^2\) and \([x_1,x_1^{\sigma_2}]\) have zero first derivative.

Thus
\[
L_{\mathrm w}=Pb+(P+\mathsf S^{-1})c
\]
and
\[
d^1_R=
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathsf S^{-1}(1+\mathsf T)&
\mathsf S^{-1}+1+\mathsf T&0&0\\
0&P&P+\mathsf S^{-1}&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This is exactly equation (5.4) of the paper after swapping its two wild
columns \(c,d\).

For a nontrivial simple module:

- if \(\mathsf T=1\), then \(P=1\), the rows force \(b=c=0\), and a
  coboundary kills \(a\);
- if \(V^{\mathsf T}=0\), then \(P=0\), the rows force \(c=0\), a
  coboundary kills \(b\), and the tame row then forces \(a=0\).

In both cases every \(H^1\)-class has a unique representative
\[
(a,b,c,d)=(0,0,0,d).
\]

For the trivial module, both relation rows have mod-\(2\) exponent vector
equal to the \(\tau\)-coordinate, so
\[
d^1(a,b,c,d)=(b,b).
\]
The scalar cup–Bockstein form on \(H^1\), in coordinates \(a,c,d\), is
\[
a\,c'+c\,a'+d\,d',
\]
which is the local Hilbert form after a change of basis.

## 5. Fox–Heisenberg duality

On the normalized representatives supported on \(x_1\), the only
second-order contributions are:

- \(x_1^2\), giving the ordinary square diagonal;
- \([x_1,x_1^{\sigma_2}]\), giving the two mixed terms involving
  \(\mathsf U\) and \(\mathsf U^{-1}\).

Consequently the degree-one pairing with
\(\lambda\in V^\vee\) is
\[
(d,\lambda)\longmapsto
\begin{cases}
\lambda(d),&\mathsf T=1,\\[1mm]
\lambda\!\left((1+\mathsf U+\mathsf U^{-1})d\right),
&V^{\mathsf T}=0.
\end{cases}
\]
In the ramified case \(\mathsf U\) has \(2\)-power order, so its minimal
polynomial divides a power of \(Z+1\).  Since
\(Z^2+Z+1\) is coprime to \(Z+1\),
\[
1+\mathsf U+\mathsf U^{-1}
=\mathsf U^{-1}(\mathsf U^2+\mathsf U+1)
\]
is invertible.  This is exactly the pairing operator in the paper.

The finite-word Stokes argument also applies.  Modulo \(2\), the exponent
vectors of the tame and wild relators are both
\[
(0,1,0,0).
\]
Their sum is zero, which is precisely the endpoint condition needed for
the degree-\(2\) trace \((u_{\mathrm t},u_{\mathrm w})\mapsto
u_{\mathrm t}+u_{\mathrm w}\).  The paper’s natural chain map is therefore
a quasi-isomorphism for simple modules.  Exact coefficient functoriality
and cone dévissage extend it to every elementary, possibly nonsplit,
module.

## 6. The quadratic obstruction

Let \(V\) be nontrivial, simple, self-dual, and let
\(q:V\to\mathbf F_2\) be an invariant nonsingular quadratic form with
polar form \(b_q\).  Evaluating Roe’s word on the normalized representative
supported on \(x_1\) gives
\[
Q_R^0(d)=q(d)+b_q(d,\mathsf U^{-1}d).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
Q_R^0(d)=
\begin{cases}
q(d),&\mathsf T=1,\\
q(d)+b_q(d,\mathsf U^{-1}d),&V^{\mathsf T}=0.
\end{cases}
\]
Indeed, in the unramified case \(\mathsf U=1\) and
\(b_q(d,d)=0\).  In the ramified case its polar form is
\[
b_R(d,d')
=b_q\!\left(d,(1+\mathsf U+\mathsf U^{-1})d'\right).
\]

These are exactly equations (6.25) and (6.26) of the paper, with the
normalized wild coordinate renamed.  Therefore the paper’s Hermitian-line
and Wall-doubling arguments give
\[
\#(Q_R^0)^{-1}(0)=
\begin{cases}
2^{n-1}-2^{n/2-1},&V\text{ unramified},\\
2^{n-1}+2^{n/2-1},&V\text{ ramified},
\end{cases}
\qquad n=\dim_{\mathbf F_2}V.
\]
These are the same zero counts, hence the same Gauss signs, as the local
Galois obstruction.

## 7. Endgame

Sections 2–6 establish for \(\Gamma_R\) all four inputs of the paper’s
complete source interface:

1. the same fully marked tame and maximal pro-\(2\) boundary;
2. the same lifting-space dimensions and lift multiplicities;
3. the same natural perfect pairings and scalar cup–Bockstein form;
4. the same quadratic Gauss sign on every self-dual simple head.

The later argument uses the source only through those inputs.  Its finite
Fourier transforms, exact-image recursion, central-cover corrections, and
induction on the normal \(2\)-subgroup of a finite target therefore give
\[
|\operatorname{Sur}(\Gamma_R,H)|
=|\operatorname{Sur}(G_{\mathbf Q_2},H)|
\]
for every finite group \(H\).  The one-sided profinite reconstruction lemma
then yields
\[
\boxed{\Gamma_R\cong G_{\mathbf Q_2}}.
\]

- [One-sided profinite reconstruction](https://roed314.github.io/gq2/paper/paper.html#lem-reconstruction)

## 8. What should be checked before calling this publication-ready

The argument above is substantially stronger than a comparison of low
finite quotients: it identifies the exact source interface used by the
paper’s all-finite-target induction.  Still, a polished proof should record
the following candidate-specific lemmas formally:

1. the crossed-derivation computation of \(\chi_R\), including the
   exclusion of the branch \(Y=X^2\);
2. the finite-word Fox ledger giving
   \(L_{\mathrm w}=Pb+(P+\mathsf S^{-1})c\);
3. the Heisenberg expansion of \(x_1^2[x_1,x_1^{\sigma_2}]\);
4. the trivial-module Stokes endpoint calculation.

Each is a short explicit identity.  No new target-induction or local
Galois-cohomology theorem appears to be needed.
